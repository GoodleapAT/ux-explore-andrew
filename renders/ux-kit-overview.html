The Kit, and the five steps

The UX Kit is a component library augmented by layers of logic. The question stops being what should a surface look like and becomes which parts does this surface need right now. The components come from the Sol design system. The logic is what turns a pile of components into something a person on another team can build with.

Three variants of the same illustration, answering the comment that grouping and order will not always be the same. A shows variation caused by lifecycle stage, B by the customer's product tier, and C states what holds and what varies instead of showing an example. One to be chosen, not all three.

Grounded in atomic design, on two axes rather than one.

Size, which is atomic design as normally understood: atoms, then molecules, then organisms, then templates and pages. Every component record names its level.

Ownership.

  • Core components are content-agnostic and know nothing about the business, contributed to by designers and engineers as a group. An object name may never appear in a core component, which is the rule that does all the work.
  • Recipes are named compositions where object names are allowed, and this is where a domain team owns its domain.
  • Snowflakes are labelled one-offs.

A snowflake becomes a recipe when a second scenario needs it, and a recipe becomes core when three do. The evidence decides, not a person.

Both axes are recorded, because how big a thing is and who owns it are different questions. A molecule can be a recipe, and an organism can be core.

Five steps.

  1. Connect the two axes. Record what each recipe is built from. This also produces the list of what Sol is missing, ordered by how much is blocked on each piece.
  2. Build the example set. Every component rendered in every state it claims, so claims and evidence can be compared.
  3. Build the coverage matrix. Components against scenarios. This is the gap list, the promotion evidence and the progress measure in one table.
  4. Settle the stage rules. What a surface shows during selling as against during install.
  5. Compose one scenario without drawing it. That is the test of whether any of this works.

A browsable component site comes last, on purpose. It is the most visible part and the least load bearing.

One caveat worth saying out loud

The words are not settled. The nouns in this work are provisional until they have been tested with contractors, and that research runs alongside the five steps.

How the library is built, and why it has a middle

The thing step one names

Atomic design gives components a size: atom, molecule, organism, template. What it does not give them is an owner. Adding that second axis is the whole of step one, and it is what lets the model keep moving without the primitives moving with it.

The entity model names things at the recipe layer and no further down, which is the same rule as the one above read from the other side, and it is what lets the model keep moving without the primitives moving with it.

Without a middle layer there are two outcomes and no third. Either the core fills up with product-specific components until nobody can use it, because the proposal card, the job card and the customer card are the same card assembled differently and now there are three of them. Or the person holding the core spends their time refusing requests with nowhere to send them. The middle exists so that a composition which is genuinely specific has somewhere legitimate to live.

Where the context system joins

The context system is a second pair of spines: jobs and the requests under them on the demand side, capabilities and the surfaces they exist on the supply side. It is not a third axis on the library. It joins in one place, on one key, and it shares the library's own promotion threshold.

A request is a proposed way to serve a job, and it is the unit the context system actually holds. A job is stable, few in number and contains no solution; a request is one idea for serving it, carrying where it came from, who it is for, what it would touch and what state it is in. Requests are meant to be cheap and disposable, and one that cannot name the job it serves is a feature request rather than a request.

What it does for people

  • A designer can name a job and see what exists, on which surfaces, and what has already been tried and declined, before drawing anything.
  • A product manager can see every request against one job with its source, so priority is argued from evidence rather than from whoever is loudest.
  • BD and sales can see what the product does today, and on which surface, before promising it.
  • Whoever is building can see which job a piece of work claims to serve, and say so when nothing answers.
  • Somebody new can read why the product is shaped this way without interviewing six people.
  • An agent can compose a concept from a job, because the context is queryable rather than remembered.
  • Everybody stops re-proposing the same idea every quarter, because declined carries a reason.
