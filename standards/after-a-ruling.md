# After a ruling

**Scope:** the minutes after Andrew settles something. Before the next question is asked.

**Surfaces:** all.

---

## How hard to hold each rule

| Marker | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Always** | Breaking it is a defect. If you must break it, say so before you do, not after |
| **Default** | Do this unless you have a reason. Having a reason is fine; not saying it is not |
| **Prefer** | A leaning. Use judgement and move on |

---

## The rules

### Always

- **Separate the principle from the mechanism, and attribute each.** A ruling usually settles a
  principle. The test that implements it is then somebody's reading, and the record has to say whose.
  Writing both as one settled thing puts words in Andrew's mouth that he did not say.
- **Sweep for what the ruling breaks before moving on.** Every ruling of any size invalidates
  something already written. Find it in the same session, because the alternative is that a document
  keeps asserting a thing that stopped being true and nobody knows which documents to distrust.
- **Sweep for what the ruling creates.** A ruling that answers one question usually opens two, and
  those two are invisible at the moment of answering because the answer feels like an ending.
- **Check whether a superseded decision was actually superseded in writing.** A new row that
  contradicts an old one without naming it leaves two live rules in one table.

### Default

- **Name the second-order consequence in the reply, not only in the document.** A consequence Andrew
  cannot see is a consequence he cannot reject.
- **Check whether the ruling dissolved the problem that prompted it.** It happens, and when it does
  the thing built in response may still be right for a different reason. **Find the other reason or
  withdraw the thing.**
- **Re-read the other scenario.** A ruling taken while working on one scenario almost always changes
  the other one, and nobody is looking at it.

### Prefer

- Ask the sweep questions out loud in the reply. They read as thinking rather than as process, and
  they are cheap.

## Worked example

**The ruling:** qualification is automatic where intent is expressed.

**What it broke**, none of which was visible when it was taken: the canonical scenario's dates, a
decision from the previous day saying the lead and the project are one to one, the other scenario's
whole intake sequence, a register row about how far apart two acts are, the presenting justification
for an object ratified four hours earlier, and a standard's worked example.

**What it created:** what closes a lead, whether a container's name may shrink, and whether
qualification is a gate or something you undo. **Three questions that were not on anybody's list**
before the answer arrived.

**The mechanism nobody ruled on:** intent was defined as a contact naming a trade and a property.
That is a reading, it decides everything about a screen, and Andrew never said it. **Recorded as a
reading, flagged for confirmation.**

**The dissolved problem:** the object created that morning was justified by a record having nowhere to
live for twenty two days. The ruling gave it somewhere to live. **The object survived on its other
argument**, which was always the stronger one.

## Why

**A ruling feels like an ending and is a beginning.** The moment after it is the cheapest possible
time to find what it broke, and the most likely time not to look, because the question that prompted
it has just been answered and attention moves on.

**And the cost compounds.** A document left asserting something false does not announce itself. The
next session reads it, believes it, and builds on it, and the error arrives wearing the authority of
the working memory.

## Learned from

- **15 Sep 2026.** Two rulings in one afternoon. A verification pass found six documents still
  asserting the replaced story after the first, and eleven more after the second, including two
  standards and the other scenario. **None of it was visible while the rulings were being taken.**
- **15 Sep 2026.** A decision of 14 September saying the lead and the project are coextensive was
  contradicted by a decision of 15 September that did not name it. **Two live Working rows, one day
  apart, in the same table**, and only a verification pass caught it.
- **15 Sep 2026.** The mechanism for a ruling was derived and written as though it had been settled.
  It was caught and attributed, but it would have been quoted back as Andrew's within a session.
- **15 Sep 2026, and this is the rule paying for itself.** A second derived inference, that one lead
  may have several projects over time, was **wrong**. It had been written as C's reading, flagged in
  the reply, and recorded as a possible divergence from the team rather than as agreement. **Andrew
  corrected it in one line and the cost was one line**: the row was withdrawn rather than superseded,
  the divergence was withdrawn, and a decision it had wrongly superseded was restored intact.
  **Had it gone in as settled, it would have been the premise of a screen.**
- **15 Sep 2026.** Working out where the wrong inference led found two real defects that the right
  answer also has: a project that can never reach a final state, and a hole in the lead's derived
  status. **The wrong path was still worth walking**, because both defects are properties of the
  model rather than of the inference.
