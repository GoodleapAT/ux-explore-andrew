Pros WebCustomer model — three viewsMockup, 26 Aug 2026

Customer, opportunity, job

Three views to test one claim: that opportunity, proposal and job can be associated rather than nested without the interface becoming unreadable. The case that matters is a customer with work in three different states at once.

[i] Inside a mockup, small grey text prefixed with [i] is my annotation, not interface copy. Everything else inside a mockup frame is intended as real UI and should be read as something a contractor would see.
Settled — carried into thisEight things already agreed ▸
  • Opportunity, proposal and job are associated, not nested. One proposal can answer several opportunities and produce several jobs.
  • An opportunity is one element of work. Siding is one, windows is another, even when they sell together.
  • A proposal contains additive scopes. Each scope has its own work, itemisation and value, and produces one job or one service.
  • A proposal with a single scope behaves exactly like today's proposal. The simple case stays simple.
  • Financing is modelled on the scope and presented at the proposal. Shared mode is the only mode built.
  • A change order attaches to one scope, because the scope is the only level that touches both the money and the work.
  • Notes and history are one event stream, filtered twice.
  • There are three product tiers: payments only, payments and selling, payments and selling and operations. Views A to C show the fullest.
The thing to decide before anything is builtPeer lists, or a hierarchy ▸

The customer view has to show three lists that are not a hierarchy. Every comparable product I have looked at resolves this by nesting one inside another, usually jobs under proposals, because nesting is far easier to draw and to scan.

Nesting would quietly reimpose the hierarchy the model is trying to avoid. The moment jobs live under proposals, three opportunities collapsing into two scopes stops being representable and the interface starts asserting something the data does not.

The views below keep the three lists as stacked peers and let the links between them carry the structure. That costs vertical space and asks more of the reader. If it does not survive contact with a real account, the honest conclusion is that the model needs revisiting, not the layout.

The row is a person, not an address

Today the list is leads, and a lead is an address. Here the row is a customer, so a second property is a "+1" beside the primary address rather than a second, unrelated record. That single change is the largest structural difference on this screen.

Counts, not a stage

A single stage column cannot describe a customer with a job in progress and a live opportunity at the same time. Counts say more and commit to less. They are also the only place the three granularities appear at this level, because names do not fit.

Only open opportunities are counted

Won and lost are history and belong on the record, not in a scanning column. What a salesperson wants from this list is where there is something left to pursue.

Value is two numbers

A recurring service has a rate, not a contract value. So the column carries an amount and, where there is one, a rate underneath. Ugly, and honest. Collapsing them into one figure would require inventing a term length.

The synopsis answers the arrival question

People open a customer record asking "where is this one". Three chunks and the last few notes answer that without scrolling, which is what earns the long page underneath. You go down only when the answer is not enough.

Contacts and properties are links, not sections

Both are peer lists that most customers barely have. Full sections would put two near-empty blocks above the work. As underlined counts in the header they stay reachable and editable without costing a screen to people who have one of each.

The stage line is the useful one

A count of completed jobs is history. What someone wants from a glance is what is happening to the work that is still moving, so the card carries the stage and its task count rather than a tally, and it is the only line given its own weight.

The primary address still shows

The header keeps a readable address and puts the count beside it. Replacing the address with "2 properties" would make the header stop identifying which house you are looking at, which is the one thing it has to do.

The balance needs a scope, and it is not obvious

Shown here against the one accepted proposal. Once a customer has two, the block has to choose: the customer's money, or this proposal's money. Those are different numbers and both are legitimate answers, which is why it is labelled rather than left implicit.

Three objects, one list

A master invoice is the total owed. A payment request is an individual ask, either drawing against a master invoice or standing on its own. A transaction is money actually moving. Different levels, but people read them as one thing in date order, so the segments filter one stream rather than opening three screens.

Four rows and a way out

A customer record is not a ledger. Enough to see whether money is moving and what is outstanding, then a link to the full history. Showing eight rows was quietly claiming this was the place to reconcile accounts.

Peers stacked, not nested

Opportunities, proposals, jobs and services are four sections at the same level. Nothing is indented under anything. The links inside each row are the only place structure is asserted, and they assert it by naming the thing on the other side.

Every section shows the live subset

Open opportunities, undecided proposals, active jobs. Everything settled sits behind a segment. The screen is for deciding what to do next, not for auditing what already happened.

Provenance is on the opportunity

Whether a customer asked, a contractor spotted it, or DeDe surfaced it changes how it gets pursued and how much it is worth chasing. Without it, three identical-looking rows get treated identically when they should not be.

Two vocabularies, so two sections

A job is scheduled, in progress, complete. A service is active, paused, cancelled. The words differ because the things differ, and one list carrying both is what made "in progress" and "active" read as the same status.

Pinned notes are facts, not events

A gate code is true until it changes. An inspection result happened on a day. Mixing them means the durable thing sinks. The stream below carries what happened, tagged with what it happened to.

Stage answers where, sub-status answers what we are waiting on

The rail is position. The waiting-on card is external processes nobody here can speed up. Keeping them apart is the whole point of the configuration model: "waiting on the city" and "someone needs to make a call" must not look the same.

A list, because ten stages will not fit across

Horizontally, ten stages either truncate or shrink past reading. Vertically they fit at full length, the board each belongs to can sit under the name, and dates line up on the right.

Two behind, two ahead, and a fade

Enough to see what just finished and what is coming without the whole pipeline competing for attention. The fade is load-bearing: it is the only thing saying the list continues, and if it reads as decoration the design has failed.

The gate is named before it bites

The blocking condition is two transitions away and already stated. Surfacing it only at the moment of refusal is what makes gates feel arbitrary, and arbitrary gates get worked around.

Recently completed is four lines, not an audit trail

Enough to confirm the job is moving and who is moving it. Anyone who needs the full record goes to the stage. A status header that scrolls has stopped being a status header.

The change order sits in the stage that caused it

Deck rot was found by a task in Installing, and the resulting change order now blocks another task in the same stage. Putting it beside the task list rather than down with the paperwork is what makes that loop legible.

Provenance is a header hint, not a card

"P‑1042, scope 1 of 2" is enough to know this job is half of something bigger, and the sheet carries the rest. The bundle is never invisible, but it never occupies the screen either, which is the balance the earlier draft missed in both directions.

Sheets are the pattern now

Contacts and properties on the customer record work the same way: a count or a phrase in the header, detail one tap away. Reusing it here means one behaviour to learn rather than three ways of reaching secondary information.

The money is borrowed, not owned

The middle card shows a figure this job cannot change alone. Stating that plainly is the point. Showing only the scope's share would read as though the job controls its own financing, which in shared mode it does not.

The change order shows both numbers

Scope value and proposal total, side by side. One tells operations what the work is now worth, the other decides whether the loan needs re-approving. Showing one without the other hides the consequence, so the card is tinted to say something is outstanding.

Placeholders are honest here

Schedule, materials, permits and invoicing are the bulk of a real job screen and none of them are affected by this model. Sketching them would invite feedback on the wrong thing.

Order is a function of tier, not a layout

Financials cannot sit sixth down the page when it is the only thing a payments customer has. The record is a composition, not a template, and the sequence has to be computed rather than authored once.

Customer means paid, not built

Becoming a Customer is having taken a payment or set up financing. That rule works identically at every tier and never mentions jobs, which makes it better than the one it replaces rather than a patch over a gap.

The balance runs ahead of the work

Accepting a proposal creates nothing to deliver at the selling tier, but it does move the amount owed. So money flows from the proposal, and a job never owns an invoice. That settles where the master invoice attaches without anyone having to prefer an answer.

Answered since this section was drawn

  • Accepting a proposal at the selling tier creates nothing, but it does change the balance.
  • A Customer is someone who has taken a payment or set up financing. Not someone with a job.
  • Tier is set per organisation, so there is no mixed-permission case inside one account.
  • The customer list gets different columns per tier, not the same columns with gaps.
  • Selling-tier contractors track the work in their own other software.
  • The payments tier is a real product and a large part of the current base, not a migration state.

What these views commit to, and what they leave alone

Carried over as-is

  • The proposal as the signed document
  • Financing bundles and one active selection
  • The existing change order path for money
  • Tiers, untouched and out of scope

New here

  • The customer as a person above the address
  • The additive scope, with its own value
  • Scope-level change orders
  • Opportunity provenance
  • Service and visit as their own objects
  • Stage, sub-status, task and gate on the job

Deliberately refused

  • Nesting jobs under proposals
  • One stage per customer
  • Per-scope financing in the UI
  • One list for jobs and services
  • A single blended contract value

Placeholder for now

  • Schedule, crew and dispatch
  • Materials and permits
  • Invoicing and payments
  • Contacts and multiple properties

What goes into the design prompts next

The screens above are the structural argument. What they do not carry is component-level detail: real table and list components, the segmented controls, expanding rows and sheets as actual disclosure patterns, empty and loading states, and how a nine-section record behaves on a narrower window.

The payments tier deserves its own round rather than a diff. This memo argues from the fullest configuration downward, which is the right way to test the model but the wrong way to design a four-section record that is already a large part of the customer base. A page where the balance is the page is a different design problem, not a subset of this one.
