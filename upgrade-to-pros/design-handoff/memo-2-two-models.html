One customer buying several things

Superseded — 28 August 2026

Model C, in which a project is the container and opportunities, proposals and jobs are peers inside it, dissolved the question this memo was asking. A versus B turned on which level is independent, and that only mattered while the proposal was assumed to be the container. It is not. Kept for the reasoning trail, in particular why the proposal cannot be the parent of jobs. Do not build from it.

A homeowner wants siding, windows and a new roof. They should receive one thing, not three. But siding and roofing are different crews, different timelines and different permits, so they are different jobs. Two models resolve that tension differently, and they are closer to each other than they first appear.

Settled — carried into thisFive things already agreed ▸
  • Bundling is decided after the quotes are ready, and whether they share financing is decided at the same moment. A proposal can still be added up until the financing application.
  • The customer receives one document, with a way through to each full proposal inside it.
  • The financed amount follows which proposals the homeowner goes forward with, not what was originally sent.
  • An opportunity is one element of work someone might buy. It survives the sale, win or lose, and it is never the thing that gets delivered.
  • Both models need a shared-or-separate financing switch, so financing mode is not a reason to prefer either.
The thing to decide before anything is builtWhich level is the independent one ▸

These are not two different data shapes. Both are three levels deep and the levels do the same jobs. Model B is Model A with the middle level promoted.

So the decision is narrow and it is structural: which level is the independent one. In Model A the priced unit lives inside a proposal and cannot leave it. In Model B the priced unit is a proposal and stands on its own. Everything else in this memo follows from that one choice.

It is worth resisting the urge to decide this on the diagrams. They look different and they are not.

A

The same three levels

The two models reduced to their levels

Only the middle column moves

The top level carries the signature and, usually, the financing. The bottom level is the work being delivered. Neither changes between the models. What changes is what sits in the middle and whether it can exist on its own.

The priced unit is where everything attaches

Opportunities attach to it, jobs derive from it, change orders land on it, and it holds the value. Promoting it to a proposal makes it independently sendable and winnable. Leaving it inside a proposal does not.

Neither model complicates the simple sale

One roof replacement is one proposal with one scope in Model A, and one proposal with no folio in Model B. Both are exactly how Pros works today. The new object appears only when there is something to bundle.

B

Model A — one proposal, several scopes

Siding, windows and a roof, sold together

A roof on its own — the case that is most sales

Two opportunities, one scope

Siding and windows collapse into one scope because one crew does both. Whether two opportunities share a scope is a question about crews, not about paperwork, and there is only one way to express it.

What it buys: the document is simple

One thing sent, one thing signed, one total, one financing arrangement. Nothing to reconcile and no ambiguity about what the customer agreed to.

What it costs: bundling must be decided first

A scope cannot be sent, tracked or won on its own. It is a second-class object by construction. A customer who wants only one of two things forces either a partial-acceptance mechanism or a reissued document.

And it fights the sale

Since bundling is decided after both quotes exist, Model A means re-authoring two finished documents into one with two scopes, at exactly the point where nobody wants to redo paperwork.

C

Model B — whole proposals, gathered into a folio

The same sale, expressed the other way

Two cases where the folio does less, or is not there at all

The folio has two lives

The proposal is the whole thing

Each has its own statement of work, itemisation and price, and each can be sent, tracked and won on its own. The folio gathers them; it does not contain them the way a document contains its scopes.

What it buys: the sale is simple

Windows gets quoted, siding gets quoted, and only then does the customer say they want both. Model B gathers what already exists. That sequence is how the conversation actually goes, which is the strongest argument in this memo.

Financing does not always move up

It stays on the proposal when only one proposal is offered, when only one moves forward, or when the homeowner finances each accepted proposal separately. It moves to the folio only when one arrangement covers everything.

What it costs: two behaviours in one object

The folio changes character partway through the sale. That is a coherent lifecycle rather than an ambiguity, but the interface has to make the transition visible rather than let it pass quietly.

And two ways to say the same thing

Siding and windows can be one proposal covering both, or two proposals in a folio. Both are legal. That is two ways to express one sale, so two ways to report on it and two ways for a contractor to get it wrong.

The case for A

The document is simple

One thing sent, one signed, one total. There is exactly one way to express any given sale, so there is exactly one way to report on it.

Its weakness is that this simplicity is internal. Answer two means Model B also produces one document for the customer, so A's advantage narrows to tidiness rather than experience.

The case for B

The sale is simple

Bundling happens after the quotes exist, which is the normal path. Model B wraps finished work; Model A asks for it to be rebuilt.

Its weakness is that the folio has to be two things at different times, and that one sale can be expressed two legitimate ways.

What this memo does not decide

It does not pick a model. Two of the three settling questions have been answered and both went Model B's way, but the choice has consequences for change orders, reporting and partial acceptance that are easier to judge against screens than against diagrams.

The next useful step is the three views drawn under Model B, so the two can be compared as interfaces rather than as structures.
