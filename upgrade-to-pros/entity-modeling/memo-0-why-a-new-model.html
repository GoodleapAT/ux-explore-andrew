What contractors sell, Merlin cannot store

A contractor sells several pieces of work in one conversation, prices them together, loses some of them, and delivers the rest on separate timelines. Merlin stores a single priced document that becomes a single job. This memo is the case that the distance between those two sentences is a modelling problem rather than a screen problem, and that it has to be closed before anything is designed on top of it.

There is no interface in this memo. One diagram, three arguments, and the outline of what any answer has to provide.

A

The sale is a bundle, the system stores one line item

What exists today · what the scenarios require

Amounts and trades follow the worked scenario used across this set: a siding and windows sale with roofing quoted and declined. The right-hand panel deliberately stops short of naming its parts, because Memo 0 argues the requirement and not the design.

What is actually sold

A contractor at a kitchen table quotes siding and roofing, raises windows unprompted, and leaves with siding and windows bought and roofing declined. That is one conversation, one price, one financing decision, and three separate fates for three pieces of work. It is the ordinary case, not the complicated one.

What is actually stored

Merlin's chain is linear and single: a customer at an address, a proposal holding exactly one scope, a job per proposal, and money hung on whichever of those created it. Every step assumes one of the thing before it. Nothing in the chain can hold a decision that has not been made yet.

The gap is two objects, not a feature

Closing it needs something to represent work that might be sold, and something to hold work sold together. Neither can be reached by adding fields to what exists, because both are cardinality changes: one becomes many, and a new level appears above the document. That is why this is a model question before it is a screen question.

B

What the absence costs, in four places

Interest has nowhere to live

Work a contractor believes is sellable, whether the customer raised it, the rep spotted it, or the system suggested it, has no record. It survives in someone's memory or a free-text note, so it cannot be counted, followed up, or reported on. A pipeline that cannot see its own earliest stage cannot be managed.

An offer cannot be varied before signing

Because a proposal holds one scope at one price, removing half of a quote means building a new proposal. The moment in the room when a customer takes two of three items is the moment the system is worst at, and it is the moment the sale is won. Everything downstream then carries a document that does not match what was agreed.

The balance is answerable nowhere

With money attached to whichever object created it, a bundled sale has its total spread across several documents and jobs. Nobody can state what the customer owes without adding it up by hand, and change orders make the sum move. This is the failure most likely to reach the customer.

Work sold together drifts apart

Two jobs from one sale run on different crews, different permits and different timelines, and they will not stay in step. With nothing above them, there is no place to see the sale as a whole, and no place a delay in one becomes visible to the person handling the other.

C

What any answer has to provide

Something has to contain the sale

All four costs in section B come from the same absence: there is no level above the document and above the job. A container solves them together rather than one at a time, which is the argument for a structural change over four separate fixes. It also has to be mandatory, because an object that exists only sometimes is one the rest of the system cannot rely on.

It cannot be the thing sent to the customer

Earlier attempts tried to make the customer-facing document do this job, and that is what pushed financing, the master invoice and the change order into awkward places. A container exists before anything is priced, changes after signing, and is never sent to anyone. Those are the properties of a workspace, not of an artefact.

Peers inside, so depth stops at two

If the parts inside the container are associated rather than nested, the simple one-job case still looks like one of everything, and the complicated case adds items rather than levels. That is the test worth applying to any proposal: does it make the ordinary sale harder in order to make the bundled one possible?

If a container is mandatory, what happens to the payments-only contractors who sell nothing through the product?

6

Where is the boundary between adding to an existing sale and starting a new one, and who decides it?

That container is the project, and the case for it is Memo 3. Three models were explored before it: two of them made the priced document the parent of the work, which is the mistake described above. Model C makes the project the container and everything inside it a peer.

Naming is a live problem rather than a detail. "Project" is already taken in Merlin as a design artefact carrying a bill of materials and an electricity profile, so the word cannot be adopted without a decision about the existing one.
