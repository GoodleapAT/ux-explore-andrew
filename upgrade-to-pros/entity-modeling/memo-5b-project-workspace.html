The project workspace, on the real design language

One section, rebuilt. This replaces section B of the statuses memo and tests two things at once: whether the status sets hold up, and whether a memo mockup can be built the way the standalone Pros Web screens are built, with cards in pairs under group headings rather than a column of full width rows. The page is the Thompsons on 9 September, with siding running and windows stuck at a permit desk.

[i] Inside the mockup, small purple italic text prefixed with [i] is my annotation, not interface copy. Everything else in the frame is intended as real UI, and the colours, type and components are Merlin's own tokens rather than an invented palette.

Taken as settled for this memo

Working assumptions, not decisions anyone has signed off

  • The project is the spine and mints on pursuit, so it exists before anything is priced. One lead per trade, and several leads can be pursued into one project.
  • Project status is derived from the proposal and the jobs. The only project status a person sets is the first one. Lead, proposal and job statuses are authored.
  • Deferred is not lost. A declined trade keeps its lead open with a reason, a note and a revive date, and keeps its priced option on the proposal.
  • Selling and work are groupings in the experience, not entities. Here they are the Sales and Operations group headings.

Groups, not a column of rows

Sales, Operations and Financials each get a heading over two cards abreast, which is how the standalone customer record is built. Eight cards fit in the height that four full width blocks took, and each pair reads as one question rather than two unrelated lists.

Segments do the work rows were doing

Leads shows Won, Deferred, Open and Lost from one card. The counts sit in the segment labels, so the card answers "what happened to demand here" before anything is clicked. My earlier version drew every list open at once, which is why it read as rows forever.

A derived status has to show its working

The rail groups seven derived states by what produces them: selling, work, then money. If a person cannot set the status they will not trust it until they can see what it came from, and the blue line above the rail is doing more work than the badge in the header.

The blocking fact has a home already

Waiting on is lifted from the job view rather than invented. Putting the permit correction there means the job row only carries a badge and a day count, and the five external processes read as one list of things nobody here can accelerate.

Two lifecycle statuses on one row

The proposal row carries Accepted, Sent and Amended by CO‑01 as three badges rather than one compound status. That is the two axes argument made in the smallest possible space, and it is the test of whether badges can hold it or whether the row needs a second line.

The uncomfortable part

The page is now dense enough that the deferred roof is a row, inside a card, inside a group, three levels below the title. On the customer record it sits near the top. Deferred demand gets quieter the further into a project you look, and no amount of layout fixes that: it is what happens when demand and commitment are separate objects.

Where this costs something

Two cards abreast is unconditional in the standalone screens, so the cards shrink rather than stacking. At memo width the four segment control on Leads is close to clipping, and it only fits because the counts are short. A fifth lead status would not fit.

Open questions

1

Does a project need a state rail at all, when its status is derived and nobody can move it? A single explained badge might be enough, and the rail invites people to try to drag it.

2

Should Waiting on be one card per project or one per job? Here one job is blocked and the other is not, and the card silently mixes both.

3

Does Leads belong on a project page, or only on the customer record? It is the only card here showing something the project does not own.

4

The rail shows the model's derived states. The configured pipeline shows the contractor's ten stages. Both describe where the work is. Which one belongs in this card, and where does the other go?

5

Three badges on the proposal row, or two badges and a second line? The row is at half width and this is where it starts to matter.
