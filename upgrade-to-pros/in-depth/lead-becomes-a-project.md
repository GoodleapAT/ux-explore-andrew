> **The question, as originally asked:** when does a lead become a project, and what does that look
> like?
>
> **Original status: open.** Six things settled on 14 September. Four questions still open, listed at
> the end.
>
> This existed to make D-01 arguable with something more specific than "at pursuit".

**The claim being tested.** A lead has a life before any container exists. Validation is real work
that produces real material. At some point a person decides to sell, and **that decision is what opens
the project**, rather than the writing of a proposal.

---

## The seven beats

### 1. Arrival

A lead exists. In the worked example, three arrive by three routes over two days: a homeowner raises
roofing in the Home App, the system suggests windows, and a person raises siding on a call.

Nothing is validated. Nobody has committed anything.

**Settled 14 Sep.** Sales **can** see unvalidated leads, because plenty of contractors have nobody
separate to validate them. In a larger organisation the sales view **hides them by default**, as a
filter rather than a permission. So it is one queue with a default, not two queues, and no handover is
required for the small case.

### 2. Validation

Somebody works it. A CSR calls, the homeowner replies, a transcript lands. The CSR confirms the
service area, confirms ownership, pulls what is known about the property, works an intake template
for the trade, and writes findings back.

**Material accrues on the lead.** Notes, transcripts, documents, investigation results. This is the
part both models are thin on: it is not a status change, it is a fortnight of accumulation.

**Nothing has been decided.** Validation can end in the lead being disqualified, and that is a normal
outcome rather than a failure.

### 3. Validated, and waiting

The lead is now worth somebody's time. It has enough on it to sell from. Nobody has committed to
selling it.

**This beat has no name in either model, and it is the one that most needs one.** Candidate wording
in the naming section below.

### 4. The moment

A salesperson takes it on. **This is the beat that decides D-01**, and there are three ways it could
work.

| | What happens | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **A · On assignment** | A manager or a routing rule assigns the validated lead to a salesperson. The project opens at that moment | No new action, no new habit, and assignment already means somebody intends to sell. But you can no longer assign a lead for a second opinion without creating a project, and reassignment gets ambiguous |
| **B · On claim** | Validated leads sit in a pool. A salesperson picks one up. The project opens when they claim it | Same virtue: the act already exists and already carries intent. Suits a team that self-serves rather than being routed. Does not suit a team where a manager allocates |
| **C · On an explicit act** | Assignment and claiming stay as they are. There is a separate act, somewhere, that means "I am going to pursue this" | Precise, and the only one where the person knows they did it. But an extra step gets skipped when people are busy and pressed when they are not, so it will be both under and over used |

**Settled 14 Sep: assignment creates the project.** A and B are the same shape, and which one a
contractor uses depends on how they run their desk, so the model does not pick. **C is not needed**:
taking a lead on is the decision, and adding a separate act after it would be asking somebody to
confirm something they have already done.

**One consequence worth seeing early.** If assignment is the trigger, then on that path **there is no
call to action at all**. The project appears because a manager routed something. The CTA only exists
on the self-serve path, where a salesperson picks up a lead themselves. So any wording chosen below
covers half the paths, and the other half needs the project to introduce itself without having been
asked for.

**The awkward case, whichever way it goes.** A salesperson takes on one lead. The same customer has
two other open leads. Does the project cover one or three?

- If taking on a lead opens a project for that lead, bundling must be a **second, later act**:
  attaching the other leads to the project that now exists.
- If taking on a lead opens a project across several, then taking on has to be a multi-select, which
  is the treatment already retired.

So the first reading is the one consistent with what was decided: **claim one, then offer the others.**
That is exactly treatment B of the pursuit moment, which is a useful confirmation, because these two
were arrived at separately.

### 5. The project on day one

**It does not need to be loud.** The salesperson is working a lead. The project is the frame around
that work rather than a place they navigate to.

It holds what selling needs and a way to mark it lost. It has no jobs, no money, no contract. It may
show nothing the lead does not already show, and that is acceptable, because its job at this stage is
to **exist**, so that the second lead has somewhere to be attached and the material has somewhere to
accumulate.

**The test for whether this beat is right:** if a salesperson never notices the project was created
and nothing they do is harder, the beat is right. If they have to learn a new concept on day one to
do the thing they were already doing, it is wrong.

### 6. The first proposal

Now the project holds something that is not on any lead: a priced offer across one or more trades.

**Whether it is a place is a function of what is in it**, and it changes more than once. Three forms,
escalating:

| Form | What it is | When |
|---|---|---|
| **1 · Implied** | No surface of its own. You are on the lead or the customer, and the container is holding your work without announcing itself | One lead, nothing priced. Beat 5 |
| **2 · A module or side pane** | Present on a page you were already on, addressable, not yet a destination | A second lead is attached, or a proposal exists. Something is in it that is not on any one lead |
| **3 · Its own page, with sub-navigation** | A destination, because there is now more inside it than fits beside something else | Jobs exist, or money exists. Beat 7 onward |

**The escalation is the advertisement.** Nobody is taught what a project is. It grows from nothing
into a side pane into a page, and each step happens because something arrived that needed the room.

Their model creates the project at this beat. Ours has it already existing and merely changing form,
which is the whole difference.

### 7. Lost, or sold

**Lost.** The project is marked lost or abandoned. Its material stays with the leads it came from, so
nothing is stranded and a revived lead next spring still carries its own history. The project is not
deleted; it is a record that a sale was attempted and did not land.

**Sold.** The contract signs, jobs appear, and the project transforms. Selling becomes history and
work becomes the subject. This is principle P-2 in action: one workspace, changing what it shows,
rather than a handover between two places.

## Naming

**The lead states**, beats 1 to 3. The question is what a salesperson sees in a queue.

| Option | Reads as |
|---|---|
| New · Validating · **Ready to sell** · Being sold | Plainest. "Ready to sell" says what is true and is an invitation rather than a label |
| New · In intake · **Qualified** · In pursuit | Familiar to anybody from a CRM, and therefore carries assumptions we may not want |
| Raised · Checking · **Ready** · Taken | Shortest. "Ready" needs the column header to carry the meaning |

I would take the first. **Ready to sell** is the one that matters, because it is the state that
invites the act in beat 4.

**The container, and what it is called on screen.** Project is decided as the entity name. The
question here is different: **what does the salesperson see, and do they ever see the word at all?**

**Proposal: the container is named by its contents, not by a person.**

> **Siding and windows** &nbsp;·&nbsp; Thompson

No naming act, so no friction at the moment somebody just wants to get on with it. It says exactly
what is in it. And when a second lead is attached, **the name grows**, which is the cheapest possible
way to teach somebody that the container exists and holds things. A person can still rename it later
if they want something like "Exterior refresh".

**This has a useful side effect.** The word Project need never appear in the interface. The entity is
called Project in the model, the screen calls it by its contents, and the breadcrumb reads Customers,
Thompson, Siding and windows. **The Merlin naming collision survives in the model and disappears from
the product**, which is worth having even though it settles nothing structurally.

**The call to action**, on the self-serve path only.

| Wording | Reads as |
|---|---|
| **Start selling** | Plain, verb first, says what happens next rather than naming an object. Best fit if the project is meant to go unnoticed |
| **Pursue** | Matches the model vocabulary exactly, and is jargon to a salesperson |
| **Take this on** | Matches the claim mental model, and says nothing about what follows |
| **Build a proposal** | Wrong. That is beat 6, and making it the trigger is precisely the position we are arguing against |

**Start selling** is the recommendation. It also reads correctly in the sentence somebody would say
afterwards: *I started selling to the Thompsons, and added the windows lead to it.*

## Advertising and showing the transition

Three separate problems, often confused.

**Advertising** is how somebody learns the container exists before they need it. Probably not at all
on day one. Two things do the work instead, and neither is an explanation:

- **The name grows.** Attach a second lead and the label goes from "Siding" to "Siding and windows".
  Somebody learns there is a thing holding both because they watched it happen.
- **The form escalates.** Implied, then a side pane, then a page. Each step happens because something
  arrived that needed room, so the container is always explaining itself by doing something useful
  rather than by being introduced.

**Triggering** is beat 4, above.

**Showing the transition** is what changes on screen when a project comes into being. If beat 5 is
right, almost nothing changes, and the transition is not an event at all. **That is the strongest
version of this argument and also the hardest to demonstrate**, because a transition nobody sees is
hard to show in a mockup.

A weaker and more drawable version: taking on a lead changes the page from a lead view to a selling
workspace that happens to be the project. Same information, more room, and the selling actions
present for the first time.

## What this settles, and what it does not

**Settles**, if the beats hold: D-01 is not undecided. The position is that a person's decision to
sell opens the container, and beat 4 says which person and which decision.

**Does not settle:**

1. **What validation actually is**: a status, a checklist, or an accumulation with no shape. Beat 2,
   and the biggest remaining hole.
2. **Whether the three forms are right**, and what exactly triggers each escalation. Beat 6.
3. **Whether a derived name survives contact with a real salesperson**, or whether people want to
   name things.
4. **What the assignment path shows**, given it has no call to action and the project simply appears.

**Settled on 14 Sep:** sales sees unvalidated leads with a default filter rather than a permission;
assignment or claim is the trigger and no separate act is needed; the container is named by its
contents; and the word Project need not appear in the interface.

**Worth noting against their model.** Their Lead can reasonably hold and reference validation
material even though the board does not draw it, so the difference is not that they have no
validation phase. It is that **nothing in their model marks the end of it**. A proposal is the first
thing that happens after validation, so validation ends when pricing starts, and the decision to
pursue has no moment of its own.
