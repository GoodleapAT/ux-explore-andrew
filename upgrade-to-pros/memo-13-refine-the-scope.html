The screen where a signed proposal becomes something a crew can build from

The contract commits a total and six lines. The crew needs twenty two. This memo draws the surface where one becomes the other, and the two states of the job page it sits between.

Five screens in the order Marcus meets them: the job at the hand-off, the refine surface on opening, part way through and committed, and the job once everything is booked. The whole sequence happens in one working day.

Settled, for the purposes of this memo

Confirmed in conversation, and carried in from memo 12.

  • The signed itemization is frozen and the job gets a working copy. The only thing that moves the signed one is a change order amending the proposal and the contract.
  • The total is owned by the contract, not by the table. Marcus can split, re-categorise and move money between lines. He cannot change the sum.
  • Per-line variance is normal and is not an error. Sold lines are round numbers built from allowances; refined lines are real quantities. They rarely match.
  • The materials list and the work order are views of the refined itemization, not documents Marcus authors separately. That is why beat 11 does all three at once.
  • The post-sale inspection stays a task for tracking, held at the project because one visit covers both trades.
  • Refining happens in a full screen overlay, not on a page of its own. It opens from the job and closes back to it, so the job page stays the only address this work has.

To decide, and it changes screen A

The rest is arrangeable.

  • How does a job show that it is waiting on a task it does not own? On 6 August the roofing job cannot refine anything until the inspection has happened, and the inspection is a project task. Drawn here as a line in the waiting-on card, which keeps your single task and avoids counting it twice across two jobs.
  • The alternative is a duplicate row on each job's checklist, which makes the count read five of five with the same thing counted twice.
  • If neither is right, the model has no way for a job to depend on anything outside itself, and that is a model gap rather than a layout choice.

The action is the one he can take

Refine the scope was the obvious primary and it is wrong on 6 August, because the inspection has not happened. Book the inspection is the only thing available, so it is the only thing offered. Booking it from either job books the one visit that covers both.

A dependency the job does not own

The inspection is a task on the project because one trip serves two trades. From the roofing job's side that makes it a thing it can only wait for, which is exactly the sub-status test. So it goes in the waiting-on line rather than the checklist, and the count stays honest at three.

The checklist rows became routes

Each row now opens the work rather than recording that it happened. Scope validated and refined is the doorway to the biggest piece of work on the page, and drawing it as a tick box hid that. The chevron is doing real work and it should be there.

Nothing is blocked, because the model has no blocked task

The first row stays open and openable. Marcus can go and look at the signed itemization right now; he simply should not refine it yet. The sequence is advised by the primary action rather than enforced by a state, which is the only option the four task states leave.

Jobs to be done, and what on the page serves them

  • J-06 Hand a signed sale to the people who will deliver it. The scope card now names what was sold as a countable thing, six lines, and offers the route into it. That is the hand-off made actionable rather than described.
  • J-05 Find out what is actually stopping a job. One line, one answer: the inspection is not booked. Before this edit the page said nothing was stopping it, which was wrong.

Open questions

  1. Should the waiting-on line say whose task the inspection is? It sits on the project and the job cannot act on it, and a reader who clicks expecting to book it from here will be right, which makes the line a route as well as a statement.
  2. Booking one visit from one job quietly acts on its sibling. Where does that get confirmed, and does the siding job show anything before it is booked?
  3. The Ops intake count reads three because HOA is not applicable. Three of four is honest about the configuration and three is honest about the work, and the tile and the card should not disagree.

He starts from the contract, not from blank

The working copy opens as a copy of what was signed, so the working total already reads $38,900 and every line already balances. Refinement adds specificity underneath figures that are already correct, which means the surface can never be in a state where the total is unexplained.

A takeover, not a page and not a drawer

There is no breadcrumb and there is a close control, which is the whole of the difference. This is one task with a beginning and an end, and it returns you to the job rather than leaving you somewhere new. A side drawer was the house pattern and it cannot hold a name, two figures and four columns at a readable width. The cost is that the job page vanishes while he works.

The inspection findings, open on arrival

Four observations and six photographs, captured on a phone two hours earlier. The strip is collapsible and it starts open here because he has just walked in with them, and closed on every later screen once he is working. One of them, the attic staining, is about to move money.

Six rows and no table

The signed side is not a spreadsheet. It is six named commitments with amounts, which is what Sara actually agreed to. The detail arrives by opening a line, not by adding columns, so the page never asks anybody to read twenty two rows before they have chosen which one they are working on.

Finish refining is offered before it is possible

Nothing is refined and the button is already there. That is the uncomfortable choice: the alternative is a control that appears only once the work is done, which is tidier and gives a first-time user no idea where this ends. Flagged rather than solved.

  1. The findings strip lives in the overlay and the job page has a notes and documents group. Is this a third place findings live, or a filtered view of the second?
  2. A takeover is a departure from the rule that detail goes sideways into a sheet. Declared here rather than assumed, and if the answer is that takeovers are allowed for one named task with a commit, that belongs in the standard rather than in a memo.
  3. Every line shows a working amount identical to its signed amount. Does that read as in balance or as not yet started? The tile says one and the rows imply the other.
  4. If Marcus refines nothing and presses Finish refining, the job is technically scope-verified with a six line itemization. Is that a legitimate outcome for a small job, or should the surface insist?

Over is a running state, not an error

Three lines are refined, three still carry their signed placeholders, and the decking line has gone up by $260. Nothing is wrong yet, because Marcus has three lines left in which to find it. The surface reports the position and does not interrupt.

One amber, in the bar

Why the signed amount stays on every row

Reopened rather than a second place

Committing closes the overlay and the job page's scope card takes over, reading 22 lines and $38,900. This is the same overlay opened again, read only, with Amend where the commit was. One surface to edit and read, one summary on the job, and no third address for the itemization to live at.

Two lines moved and the movements cancel

Decking went up by $260 and site protection came down by $260, because a third cleanup day on a two day install was a sales estimate rather than a plan. The total did not move, so no change order, no conversation with Sara, and the contract is untouched.

What this produces is the point of the screen

Three things fall out of one act, which is why the script does all three in one sitting. The materials list is the materials category with quantities. The work order is the services category plus the scope text and the access notes, with the prices stripped, because a crew lead does not need the sell price and should not see it.

The category tags were doing work all along

Every refined row carries Materials, Equipment or Services in its status line. It looks like metadata and it is the sort key. Those three words are what make one table produce two documents, and they are why the category has to be chosen per line rather than per section.

Still no cost anywhere

These are sell prices. A reader would reasonably expect this to be where margin gets worked out and it is not, because cost lands on the job and revenue at the project and no allocation rule exists. That absence is louder here than anywhere else in the set.

Jobs to be done, and what on the page serves them

  • J-06 Hand a signed sale to the people who will deliver it. Completed. Six commitments became twenty two instructions and three documents, and the coordinator stopped holding any of it in his head.
  • J-10 Start using the product without adopting all of it. A payments-only contractor never sees this surface, and a selling-tier one sees it without the work order. The category tags are what let the same table serve all three tiers.

Open questions

  1. Is the work order a record or a view? For an internal crew everything in it is already on the job page. For a subcontractor it carries its own labour price and its own visibility boundary, which makes it a record. The same name covering both is how the cross-organisation problem stays hidden.
  2. The two deltas cancel and the screen says so quietly. Does anybody need to approve that a coordinator moved $260 between lines, or is a fixed total authority enough?
  3. Where does this itemization live? If the job owns it, the project holds two for two trades and they must reconcile to one contract. If the proposal owns it, a signed document has a mutable child.
  4. Twenty two rows on one screen with every line open. Is committed the state where everything collapses, and if so the job page's scope card becomes the only place anybody reads this.

The scope card is now a summary of a real document

Six lines became twenty two and the card says so, with the route beside it. The two numbers on that line, 22 and $38,900, are the whole of the refinement story compressed into somewhere a reader will actually look.

The work order appears where it is produced

It sits under Crew, not under Documents, because it is the instruction that went with the assignment. For an internal crew it is a view of this page and putting it beside the crew is what makes that obvious. For a subcontractor it would need its own money and its own visibility, which this row hides.

Six notes, and one of them changed a number

The attic staining note carries its consequence in its own sub line. A note that moved money should not read the same as a note about where to park, and the only device used here is the sentence itself, which is the first rung of the fallback ladder.

Still no buttons

Everything is done and nothing is due until Sunday. The page has no action and no warning colour, which is the correct reading of a job that is simply waiting for its date. Adding a control here would be inventing work.

Jobs to be done, and what on the page serves them

  • J-05 Find out what is actually stopping a job. One line, the materials, with a date four days before the crew. Everything else on the page is settled, and the page does not pretend otherwise.
  • J-06 Hand a signed sale to the people who will deliver it. Finished and evidenced. Scope, access, crew, dates and a work order that went out, each with the date it happened.
  • J-07 Walk into a conversation knowing what was said last time. Six notes across three sources. The 12 August call still has no entity, so what a reader sees is Marcus's summary of it.

Open questions

  1. The work order is a row under Crew and also, arguably, a document. Listing it in both places is the honest reading and looks like a duplicate, and picking one hides half of what it is.
  2. Photographs now come from two sources with different purposes, sales assessment and ops inspection. Eighteen is a count of two different things, and a crew looking for the attic photo has to know which.
  3. Final checks now shows Mon 17 Aug because the scenario gained a day-before beat, so the rail reports rather than predicts. Installing still shows Tue 18 Aug, which is a prediction, and the rail does not distinguish the two.
  4. Both jobs read 6 of 6 and Ready at the same moment, so the sibling card is currently decoration. It earns its place on 18 August, when roofing is in closeout and siding has not started.

The schedule, revised 17 September

Three beats go in and one moves. The inspection is beat 10a, booked on the Thursday afternoon and carried out at 8am on Friday 7 August. Marcus is back at his desk by 11:20, refines through the middle of the day and files the permit that afternoon. Beats 11 and 14 already both sat on 7 August and the script says the permits were filed "in the same sitting that built the work orders", so that day was always meant to hold all of it.

The install week gains its day-before checks and loses a day of siding. Monday 17 August is checks on the roofing job, roofing runs Tuesday 18 to Wednesday 19, siding is checked on the Wednesday and runs Thursday 20 to Friday 21. Each job gets its own check rather than the project, which is the right reading, because the gate is on the job's own transition into Installing.

Nothing downstream moves. Both jobs still complete on Friday 21 August, the invoice is still Monday the 24th and payment is still Wednesday the 26th, so the three day amber window between completion and invoicing survives. It is the only amber in the scenario.

Two costs, both worth saying out loud. Siding drops from three days to two, which for 2,140 square feet of fibre cement with a crew of three is tight, and anybody who builds for a living will say so. And beat 18's canonical screen day moves from Tuesday 18 to Wednesday 19 August, which is still the only day where one job is complete and the other is merely scheduled.

Separately, memo 11 dated the post-sale inspection 10 August, three days after the refinement it is supposed to produce. That memo is now stale on this point, as is memo 12's install week.
